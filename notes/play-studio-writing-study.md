# Play Studio writing study

Reviewed September 7, 2026. Opened all 32 project pages linked from [Play's work index](https://play.studio/work), read their available case-study copy, and examined Driver's narrative in the live page layout. This is an editorial analysis of published case studies, not verification of the clients' product or outcome claims. Website copy remains unchanged.

## Principal finding

The strongest case studies make design decisions legible. They establish an audience or business problem, introduce a central idea, and show how particular choices express that idea. The writing gives readers a reason to look closely at the adjacent work.

There is no single uniform Play format. Some pages contain only an introductory paragraph and a visual portfolio. Others unfold over several explanatory sections. Use the detailed cases as the model for demonstrating judgment; the shortest cases are useful for learning compression, but provide little evidence of research or iteration.

Their published narratives are also curated accounts. A coherent explanation of a finished identity does not establish that the concept came first, that alternatives were tested, or that a claimed effect was measured. Do not invent those steps when adapting the approach to a personal portfolio.

## Writing mechanics to carry into the portfolio

1. **Open with the situation.** Explain what the product enables, who it serves, or what has changed. Give the design assignment context before listing deliverables.
2. **Name the actual difficulty.** Examples include conflicting audiences, existing brand equity, unfamiliar behavior, data complexity, or constraints on tools and assets.
3. **Introduce one organizing idea.** It should explain several decisions. Avoid attaching an unrelated metaphor to every section.
4. **Explain a mechanism.** Identify the visible choice, why it was appropriate, and what it enables. A named typeface alone is trivia; its behavior or role in the system is useful reasoning.
5. **Alternate explanation with evidence.** Let a short passage direct attention to the adjacent image, sequence, or interaction. Avoid a long essay that the visuals cannot substantiate.
6. **Use active, concrete verbs.** Grouped, retained, removed, paired, tested, reordered, and adapted carry more information than elevated, transformed, or reimagined on their own.
7. **Vary rhythm without fragmenting every sentence.** Play uses short emphatic sentences and fragments around longer explanations. Borrow the rhythm selectively so the causal chain remains easy to follow.
8. **Separate scope from the idea.** A role/services line can state product, brand, campaign, and event work. The main paragraph should explain what held that work together.
9. **Keep attribution precise.** Their studio voice uses “we.” A personal portfolio should identify what the author owned, what was collaborative, and what existed beforehand.
10. **Distinguish intention from results.** “Designed to help people compare options” is different from claiming a measured increase in confidence or conversion. Use measured outcomes only with evidence.

Some Play passages use promotional superlatives, familiar innovation language, and broad outcome assertions. Those are less useful for this portfolio than their concrete explanations. The objective is original copy with comparable clarity, specificity, and narrative discipline.

## Proposed editorial structure for our project pages

This is a recommendation, not a claim that all Play pages follow these lengths.

- **One-line descriptor:** 10–20 words identifying the project and the contribution.
- **Main introduction:** roughly 80–120 words covering context, audience difficulty, central approach, and scope.
- **Section descriptors:** roughly 40–80 words each. One decision or connected set of decisions per section.
- **Optional outcome:** a specific observed result, learning, or honest statement of what the resulting system enables.

A useful drafting scaffold is: **Situation → audience insight → decision → execution → consequence.** These are questions to answer in natural prose, not mandatory visible headings.

For each section, ask: What did we learn? What did that change? What can the reader see here that demonstrates the change? What result can we support?

## Arcade: audit of the existing material

Source: `design-content.js`, Arcade project. The short descriptor currently identifies product, brand, and event work; the main `intro` is empty. Existing narrative sections are New York Consumer Launch, Holiday Campaign, and Composer.

### Strongest material already present

- **Composer:** the current account links research and iteration to browsing, comparison, and steering. This is a behavioral insight with a visible interface consequence. It could anchor the larger story, provided the research and ownership are specified.
- **Consumer launch:** the before/during/after structure links prompting, physical objects, and a gathering of creatives. This can show how a product experience became an event experience.
- **Holiday campaign:** demonstrating creation and editing gives the campaign a teaching role. Material and making-process imagery can connect that lesson with the appeal of the finished object.

### What needs clarification

- Who was the primary audience during this work? Artists, interior designers, shoppers, and AI newcomers are not automatically the same segment.
- What specifically did Aayush own? Do not imply authorship of Arcade's original logo, identity, or entire strategy from the presence of those assets in the portfolio.
- What observations informed the product decisions? Which earlier approach was insufficient, and what changed after research?
- Which visual choices were deliberate and personally shaped: typography, image selection, texture, material cues, layout, or motion? Existing media alone does not prove their rationale.
- What happened after launch or iteration? Identify usable observations or results without converting intentions into measured outcomes.

### Editing priorities

- Replace internal shorthand such as ICP with the actual audience.
- Qualify broad statements about what all shoppers want by grounding them in the audience observed.
- Make the reason for each decision explicit rather than relying on phrases such as craft becoming a lesson.
- Give the introduction a single thread connecting the work; leave detailed launch mechanics and campaign explanations to their own sections.
- If supported, consider leading with the Composer work before campaigns so readers understand the creation behavior that the brand and launch are teaching. The current page is not arranged in that causal order. This is a future editorial option, not an implemented change.

### Working direction, requiring confirmation

The supplied material suggests a possible central idea: **helping people discover what they want to make, and making the route from an idea to a physical object feel approachable.** That can connect browsing and steering in the product, demonstrations in the campaign, and the physical experience of the launch.

This is an interpretation of the current copy, not a confirmed brief or a final descriptor. Before drafting in first person, establish the audience problem, Aayush's actual ownership, and the key visual/interaction decisions. The main descriptor should then introduce that relationship in plain language, supported by the three existing chapters.
