# Arcade: narrative and design reading

Working draft, September 7, 2026. Not published to the portfolio.

## Confirmed context

- Broad ownership across the work; typeface and color palette were inherited.
- Design-conscious, mass-affluent shoppers were often unfamiliar with AI and with the possibility of creating physical products through it.
- A smaller creator group supplied ideas and products; the larger audience discovered, followed, and shopped. Creators also brought their own audiences.
- Education, useful starting points, and tastemaker social proof were deliberate priorities.

## Main descriptor — proposed

Arcade turns ideas into physical products. For design-conscious shoppers unfamiliar with AI, that meant introducing a new way to shop and create. The challenge was helping people discover what they wanted to make, and making the route from an idea to a physical object feel approachable.

Building on an established typeface and palette, I led design across the product, brand, campaigns, and events. We gave people starting points: a room to match, a design to adapt, a collection to explore. Campaigns paired prompting with the materials and settings of the finished object. Artists and tastemakers showed what was possible, bringing their audiences into a marketplace where people could create, customize, or simply find something they loved.

## Evidence and interpretation

These readings explain plausible purposes of visible choices. They are not claims about measured outcomes or confirmation that every rationale was part of the original brief.

### Start with taste

The pillow creation screen offers a photo upload, Match Color, Match your room, and existing designs. The prompt panel offers style chips and product thumbnails. These give the shopper several ways to express a preference before composing an original prompt. Familiar interests become inputs.

### Make exploration part of creation

The pillow results screen combines conversation with a browsable grid, prices, filters, favorites, and editing. The Dreamboards/My Creations screen stores collections and previous outputs. Together they support comparison and revision rather than asking one prompt to deliver a final answer. The visible mockups contain placeholder copy, so they should not be described as evidence of shipped outcomes without confirmation.

### Explain the capability through the object

The /edit campaign pairs two ring variants with an instruction; /dream pairs a prompt with an engraved bucket. Neighboring images place the products on fabric, wood, and a table setting. My reading: instruction explains how to participate, while context helps the audience imagine owning the result.

### Give AI outputs physical credibility

Material choices such as hand-knotted, hand-tufted, and printed; product prices; and material-rich imagery connect creation to buying an actual object. Cyanotype-like imagery evokes physical impressions and surfaces. These choices plausibly address uncertainty about what the platform can make, but they do not establish manufacturing quality or customer trust by themselves.

### Make creators part of the product

The rug card includes a creator handle, and the pillow results include Dreamer/Maker attribution. Combined with the user-confirmed tastemaker strategy, this supports a narrative about creators providing inspiration, social proof, and distribution. The inspected careers page contains people and partner proof, but it is not evidence of the consumer homepage layout.

### Let the waitlist begin the experience

The rug waitlist asks for an idea against a recognizable product silhouette. It invites a small act of creation before launch. This plausibly makes the future capability concrete; no conversion lift has been established.

## Suggested story sequence

1. Make an unfamiliar possibility understandable: main introduction.
2. Give people a place to start: category, photo, and reference flows.
3. Let taste develop through browsing: Composer and collections.
4. Teach through desire: /dream and /edit campaigns.
5. Bring creators and their audiences together: launch and marketplace attribution.

Do not claim authorship of the inherited typeface/palette, universalize the approximate creator/shopper split, or claim conversion, retention, trust, or market leadership outcomes without supporting evidence.
